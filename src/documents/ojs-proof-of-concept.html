Should we adopt OJS and/or OPS — and what about the Research Hub?

Two systems are on trial, and a third is already publishing the agency's research. This is the page where the three meet: what each is for, where the test stands, what adopting each would take, and what the evidence says to do.

Part one · OJS

OJS: a journal, expressed as software

Open Journal Systems is free software for running a peer-reviewed journal, made by the Public Knowledge Project, a non-profit, and used by tens of thousands of journals. It collects submissions, sends them to reviewers, tracks the rounds and the edits, and publishes the finished articles in issues. Everything about it follows from that: you create a journal before you can create anything else, content is filed into volumes and issues, people hold journal roles, and the finish line is an issue with a table of contents. Running a peer-reviewed periodical is the one job it should be judged on, and that is the job this part judges.

What OJS does

1

Submit

An author uploads a manuscript through a web form. No email attachments, no lost drafts.

2

Review

Editors assign reviewers. Everyone sees deadlines and status on one screen.

3

Edit

Revisions, copyedits, and layout happen in one place with a full history.

4

Publish

Articles go live on a public website, organized by issue, searchable and citable.

Without a system like this, journal work runs on email threads, shared folders, and people remembering who has what. OJS puts all of it in one place with a record of every step.

What it would give R&A

A peer-reviewed quarterly, beside the Hub

The trait that rules OJS out as a replacement for the Hub — that it is built to produce a periodical, in issues, on a schedule — is what would make it worth having as an addition. Part three makes the case against replacement in full; this box is about the addition.

The Hub already does the thing OJS does poorly: publishing individual pieces, continuously, each one as it is finished. It should carry on doing exactly that. What the Hub does not do, and cannot easily be made to do, is gather that work into a formal, peer-reviewed volume that the scholarly world recognizes as such.

So picture a quarterly. Nothing about the Hub changes:

  • Research keeps appearing on the Hub as it always has — article by article, as each is ready, reaching the readers it already reaches.
  • Four times a year, a selection is assembled into a reviewed issue of an ICJIA journal: an editorial board, outside reviewers, an ISSN (the standard number identifying a periodical, as an ISBN identifies a book), and a DOI on every article.
  • The two are not rivals. The Studio already has a box for a DOI, so the Hub can list the article carrying the identifier the journal issued. A reader finds the work either way, and the citation points somewhere permanent. Nothing new has to be built for that; the box is already there.

Why this could matter to R&A. Hub articles are widely read — the figures in part three make that plain — but they are not peer-reviewed, carry no DOI of their own, and are largely invisible to Google Scholar, citation counts, and library catalogs. That is a real ceiling on work the agency has already paid to produce. A quarterly would put a portion of it over that line: formally reviewed, permanently identified, properly indexed, and citable as scholarship rather than as a government web page. It would also give the unit a publishing rhythm and a standing invitation to outside authors, which the Hub's continuous model never creates on its own.

The costs are real and are set out next: a second system to run, keep updated, back up, and staff — and, harder than any of that, an editorial board and reviewers willing to do the work.

What it would actually cost. The software stays free, but a real journal is not a $0 line item. Part two · OPS

OPS: the same machine, one stage

Open Preprint Systems is OJS's sibling, built from the same code by the same non-profit, for posting finished research straight away. An author submits, a moderator checks that the piece belongs, and it is posted with a date stamp — no reviewers, no rounds, no copyediting, no issues. Everything it holds it labels a preprint, permanently and in a form machines read. What is OJS? What is OPS? has the full account; this part asks only whether ICJIA should adopt it.

What is running

OPS was installed on 6 September 2026 on the same server as the OJS test — its own database, its own address, its own logins. Because the journal's problems were already solved, it took twenty minutes and hit none of them. It is installed and sending email; nothing has been posted to it. Before anyone is given a login, one thing to know: accounts do not carry across. OJS and OPS keep separate user tables, so whoever administers both needs an account on each, created separately and kept in step by hand.

That is what is installed. The rest of this part is whether to keep it.

What it would add beside the Hub — and what it would not

Less than you would hope

The Hub already posts research continuously, each piece when it is finished, after one person's approval. So does OPS. That overlap is the difficulty: OPS is not a strange new category but a near-copy of something ICJIA already owns, and a near-copy is much harder to argue for than something different.

  • What OPS adds: versions that stay citable — a revision goes up as a new version and the old one stays reachable; a DOI for each preprint, given a Crossref membership; and indexing as scholarship, so the work becomes visible to Google Scholar and the tools that count citations.
  • What it gives up: datasets and dashboards as publications (a dataset becomes a file attached to something else; a dashboard has nowhere to live), the Hub's readers and its ranked web addresses, and an accurate description of the work — OPS would describe ICJIA's finished, authoritative research as provisional, permanently.
  • What it costs: a second continuous-posting system to patch, back up and staff, in exchange for versioning and indexing that a smaller change to the Studio might also deliver — part three costs that alternative.

Where OPS earns its place

As the front half of a journal

The strongest case for OPS is not publication at all. If ICJIA runs the quarterly described in part one, there is a real gap between “a working paper exists” and “an issue comes out six months later.” OPS fills exactly that gap: the paper is posted, dated, versioned and citable straight away, and when the reviewed version appears in the journal, the preprint points at it. That is the job OPS was built for, and the one place it is clearly the right tool.

So OPS depends on the journal. If the quarterly does not happen, the case for OPS largely goes with it.

OPS, in one line: keep it installed for the length of this test — it costs nothing extra to run and it is the cheapest way to see the workflow for ourselves — but do not treat it as a separate decision. Decide the journal first. OPS is a sensible companion to a quarterly and hard to defend without one.

Part three · The Research Hub

The Research Hub: what either decision has to reckon with

ICJIA already publishes research, through the Research Hub and the Studio behind it — the tool authors use to create and publish their work. A declared interest: the author of this page helped build the Hub and wrote Hub 2.0, so this part argues only from figures and documents anyone can check. Two versions are in play: Hub 1.0 and Studio 1.0, live since 2019, and Hub 2.0 and Studio 2.0, the security update and rewrite, drafted but not yet live. The beginner's guide is the full account; this part keeps only what the decision needs.

Hub 1.0 — live now

The public site at icjia.illinois.gov/researchhub, live since 2019. It publishes articles, datasets, and interactive dashboards.

It is the most-read part of ICJIA's website — 47% of all pages read and 60% of all visitors over the past year. Eight of the twelve most-visited pages on the whole site are Hub articles.

Its weak point: the software that stores its content stopped getting security updates in 2022. Hub 1.0 has run its course.

Studio 1.0 — behind it

The staff tool behind Hub 1.0, where authors create and publish. R&A writers work in a simple text editor, see the page exactly as it will appear, attach files, and save a draft.

Work moves created → submitted → published. A manager looks at what's waiting and publishes it. The public Hub has no editing features at all; every change starts in the Studio.

The catch: review is a single step — one approval, with no reviewer comments kept on the record.

Hub 2.0 and Studio 2.0 — draft finished, not yet live

The security update and rewrite of both, on a current content system, with Studio 2.0 a significant upgrade over Studio 1.0. The draft is finished but not yet live, pending testing and approval, much like this test. Working drafts are up for internal review: the public site (opens in a new tab) and Studio 2.0 (opens in a new tab) (choose “editor”; no password needed). The drafts carry the internal codename “Copperhead”; readers never see it.

This proof-of-concept doesn't affect it either way.

What the Hub has already earned

Over the twelve months to early September 2026, by the site's own analytics: 34,800 of icjia.illinois.gov's 58,300 visitors read the Hub (60%), along with 47% of all pages read; 84% of the visitors Google sends the whole site arrive on a Hub page; a Hub visit averages just over fifteen minutes against ten for the site; and eight of the twelve most-visited pages on the site are Hub articles. That standing is attached to roughly 540 web addresses that Google has ranked for seven years. Move a page and, to a search engine, it is a new page that has to be found and judged again, a process measured in months, with every link, bookmark and citation to the old address affected at once. The full table, its source and the content counts are in the beginner's guide.

Could OJS or OPS replace the Hub and Studio outright?

No

OJS is a journal, expressed as software. You create a journal before anything else, content is filed into issues and sections, everyone holds a journal role, and nothing publishes until there is an issue to publish it into. OPS has no issues, but it labels everything it holds a preprint, permanently and in a form machines read, so finished, authoritative research would be described as provisional. In either system a dataset becomes a file attached to something else (in OJS that file can at least carry its own DOI), and a dashboard has nowhere to live at all.

Then there is the cost of moving. Either system builds its own address structure, so all of the Hub's roughly 540 pages would move; forwarding carries much of the standing across, but only if every address is mapped and kept forwarding permanently, and the climb back still takes months. And off-the-shelf software is still a server to run, so the in-house platform would survive anyway, reduced, for the dashboards and datasets that cannot leave, next to a second system. The beginner's guide works through what getting rid of the Hub would mean in full.

The non-profit behind both, the Public Knowledge Project, hit this same limit itself and answered it with sister tools: OPS for preprints, and OMP for books and monographs. For a library of everything an agency has produced, the category is repository software such as DSpace. None of those is proposed. OJS is the industry standard for running a peer-reviewed periodical, and that is the one job it should be judged on, which is what part one did.

Could the Studio be built out to do what OJS does?

Much of it — but not peer review, and not the DOI

Versioning, the labeling Google Scholar reads and a feed for library catalogs are ordinary additions to something that already exists, days to weeks each, costed item by item in the beginner's guide. Two things do not yield to that. Peer review is a product, not a feature: reviewers, rounds, deadlines, reminders and hidden names are months of work maintained forever after, and it is what OJS already is, refined over twenty-five years. It is the one thing not to build. A DOI is a commitment, not a feature: the membership, the annual fee, the identifier registered in ICJIA's name and the permanent promise to keep every number working are the same whichever tool sends the request. Adopting OJS does not buy ICJIA a DOI, only a well-tested way of registering one.

The counterweight: anything built into the Studio, ICJIA maintains forever, while the review machinery inside OJS is maintained by the non-profit that makes it. Building our own trades a hosting burden for a development burden. Neither is free.

What Hub 2.0 changes, and what it doesn't

Hub 2.0 moves the Hub onto current, supported software, stands it on its own instead of inside the main website's code, and keeps every existing web address working, so the moving cost above never arises. Studio 2.0 lets a reviewer highlight a passage and leave a comment that stays with the draft: a better conversation than today's single approval, not peer review. What it does not change is the publishing model. The same three kinds of content, posted continuously, approved by one person; for readers, the same Hub in a modern interface, with nothing added and nothing removed. The OJS and OPS write-up sets Hub 2.0 beside both in one table, and the beginner's guide sets out what it changes and what it does not.

The Hub, in one line: its job is getting research read, and neither system on trial can take that job over. Hub 1.0 has run its course; Hub 2.0 is the rebuild waiting to replace it. What OJS and OPS can add is a scholarly record on top of whichever Hub is running.

Three decisions, not one

Three questions get mixed together in conversation. They are separate, they have different answers, and running them together as one yes-or-no is how the wrong answer gets given.

  • Release Hub 2.0? Ready to decide. It fixes the unsupported-software problem and keeps the readers, the addresses and the content. It does not change who maintains the code, and it is not live until it has been tested and approved. It can be decided on its own, whatever is decided about the rest.
  • Start a journal on OJS? Being tested — this page and this server exist to inform it. The software question is nearly answered; the editorial one is R&A's.
  • Add OPS? After the journal. A sensible companion to a quarterly and hard to defend without one.

The question for R&A isn't which tool. It's whether ICJIA wants to publish a peer-reviewed journal — a quarterly of the agency's own research, reviewed and citable, on top of the Hub rather than instead of it. If that is worth having, OJS alongside the Hub is the cheapest correct way to get it, and this test server is the sensible way to find out. If what's actually wanted is more rigor on Hub content, that belongs in the Studio, not in a second system. Either way the software is the easy part: the editorial board, the unpaid reviewers, and the steady stream of submissions are what a test server can't tell us we have.

If the answer is yes: what has to happen first

Five things would need to happen before OJS could host a real journal. None of them is difficult, and each has a clear owner. If the preprint server is kept too, each of the five covers both applications rather than one.

A bigger server

The test machine is deliberately tiny. A real journal needs more room. On-premises is preferred, run by someone in R&A or IDS with remote access. AWS is a workable alternative; the agency has run a server there before.

Decision: IDS

Linux or Windows

OJS is proven on Linux. Windows Server is possible but unfamiliar territory and would need its own evaluation. It might even be quicker to stand up, which is not a recommendation: the case for Linux is that it is what OJS and OPS are built, tested and supported on.

Evaluation: IDS

An official address

We have a contact at DoIT who can create ojs.icjia.illinois.gov. Moving the site from the test address is routine.

DoIT request

Approved email

Sending from an illinois.gov address means DoIT must authorize the email service. Best raised together with the address request. The test uses the mail service ICJIA already uses for its other websites, because it was already set up and is now verified; production can use any provider DoIT approves.

DoIT request

A verified backup and restore plan

The test server gets a full weekly copy from the hosting provider, plus a nightly copy of each application's database and files kept on the machine itself, which is fine for a throwaway. A live journal is an archive of record: it needs enterprise-grade backups with frequent incremental copies, off-site storage, and, most importantly, regular test restores so we know the backups actually work before we ever need them.

Count the applications when scoping this. Two applications mean two databases and two sets of uploaded files. A backup plan that covers one of them and is assumed to cover both is the failure that actually happens, and it is silent until the day it matters.

Plan: IDS

Why this test setup is low risk

No license to buy

OJS and OPS are open source. The test server costs a few dollars a month and can be switched off any time. Production is not free — part one says what it would actually cost.

Nothing to lose

The test lives at temporary addresses (ojs.icjia.cloud and ops.icjia.cloud) with made-up content. Nothing is published, indexed, or permanent.

Well-trodden ground

OJS has been in development since 2001 and runs on ordinary web hosting, the same kind IDS already manages for ICJIA's other sites.

Same hands

The test is being built by IDS, which already runs 15+ ICJIA web properties. This is a smaller job than most of them.

This server is not permanent. This is a proof-of-concept test.

A journal article carries a DOI, a permanent identifier that libraries, citations, and search engines rely on forever. A DOI cannot be recalled once issued.

If anything were published from this test server, the world would point to a temporary address on a machine we intend to delete. No DOIs will be issued, and nothing will be indexed or announced, until a production system exists.

Nothing on the test server is real, published, or permanent.

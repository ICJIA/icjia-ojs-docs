Running the preprint server

Everything the OPS moderator does, from first login to posting a preprint — all of it in a web browser.

Last updated6 September 2026

You do not need a server login, and you will not be given one. If a task has a settings screen, it is yours. If it needs someone to log in to the machine itself, it is a ticket.

Before you start

Two applications are installed on the test machine, and they were two separate installations. Open Journal Systems runs at one web address and Open Preprint Systems (OPS) at another. Each has its own database, its own folder of uploaded files, and its own user accounts. Installing the second did not extend the first, and nothing you change in one appears in the other. This guide covers the second one.

The journal has its own guide: Running the journal. Two further documents cover the rest: what OPS is and how it differs from OJS and the Research Hub, and a server runbook for the developers who build the machine.

This guide is written for the person moderating the preprint server. It assumes no server knowledge and asks for none. It also assumes you have not read the journal guide cover to cover, and says which parts of it to borrow rather than repeating them here.

One word to watch, before anything else. In OPS, “server” means a preprint server — a publication, and the exact counterpart of a journal in OJS. It is the thing you create in step 2, and it is what the role Preprint Server Manager refers to. The other kind of server, the computer everything runs on, is the one you do not need a login for. Every use of the word below is the first kind unless it says otherwise.

While this is a test, nothing you do here is permanent, posted, or indexed. Break things freely — that is what a test server is for.

What carries over from the journal guide

OJS and OPS are built by the same people from the same code, so most of the screens are the same screens. That is why this guide is short: where the journal guide already covers something, it is linked rather than restated, and the two cannot drift apart.

Read the journal guide for these

Identical in OPS. Same screens, same wording, same answers.

  • Adding users and changing their roles. Users & Roles → Users → Add User, exactly as described there.
  • Editing email templates, themes, and pages, under Settings.
  • Switching on add-ons from the plugin gallery.
  • Try it first, or file a ticket? The dividing line is the same one: if the fix is in a Settings menu it is yours, if it needs a login to the machine it is a ticket.
  • The quick email check before reporting anything about mail, including the 15-minute wait and the [EXTERNAL] tag the state's mail system adds.
  • Filing a useful ticket, and what to look at when something breaks.

Ignore the journal guide for these

Absent from OPS. Not hidden, not switched off — not there.

  • Reviewers. There is no review stage to invite anyone into, so no rounds, no deadlines, and no anonymity settings.
  • Editors and Section Editors. The editorial decision those roles exist to make does not happen here.
  • Copyediting and production. One stage covers the whole journey.
  • Issues and volumes. Nothing waits to be gathered into anything; a preprint goes up on its own.
  • Step 6 of that guide — running one article through review. The equivalent test here is a different shape, and it is the section after next.
The one-line difference: OJS moves a submission through four stages and publishes it inside an issue. OPS has one stage, one person screening it, and posts it on its own. Everything else on this page follows from that.

Getting started

Work top to bottom. It is a shorter list than the journal's, and step 5 is the one people skip and then wish they had not.

  1. 1

    Log in and secure the admin account

    Sign in with the username and password IDS gave you — the ones for OPS, which are not the OJS ones even if you have both. Top-right menu → Edit Profile → Password and set your own. Then Profile → Contact and check the email address is one you actually read; every notification goes there.

  2. 2

    Create the preprint server

    A fresh OPS site has no servers on it, the way a fresh OJS site has no journals. Administration → Hosted Servers → Create Server. Give it a title, a short web path (this becomes part of every link, so keep it short and never change it later), and a language.

    OPS then opens the Settings wizard. Fill in Masthead and Contact — principal contact is you, technical contact is IDS. Everything else can wait.

    Leave off during the test: Settings → Distribution → DOIs, and anything labelled indexing or search-engine registration. OPS can issue a DOI of its own, and a DOI cannot be withdrawn once issued. Nothing on a temporary address should carry one.

  3. 3

    Learn the roles before adding people

    Shorter than the journal's list, because the editorial roles have nothing to do here. The four that matter:

    RoleWhat they can doWho gets it
    Site AdministratorEverything, on every preprint server, plus site settings and creating servers.You, and one backup (step 4).
    Preprint Server ManagerAll settings, users, and workflow for one server. The counterpart of Journal Manager in OJS.Whoever runs the server day to day — often you.
    ModeratorSees every submission; checks it is in scope and appropriate; posts it or declines it.The person doing the screening. This is the working role.
    AuthorSubmits, tracks, and posts new versions of their own work.Anyone who registers.

    A moderator is not an editor. The job is to confirm the piece belongs here and is fit to be public — not to judge whether the research is sound. That judgement is what peer review is for, and OPS deliberately does not do it.

  4. 4

    Add users, and name a backup administrator here too

    Add people as the journal guide describes; the screen is the same. Tick Send the user a welcome email so they get their login, which also proves outgoing mail is working from this application.

    The trap: a backup administrator on OJS is not a backup administrator on OPS. Create the second admin in each, and tell IDS both usernames. The last section on this page says why.

  5. 5

    Write down what the server accepts

    This is the step with no equivalent in the journal guide, and the one that makes moderation possible. A moderator screens for scope — so somebody has to have written the scope down. Put it in the server's description and in the submission checklist, under Settings.

    Answer three things in plain language: what subject matter belongs here, what stage a piece has to be at to be posted (finished work, not drafts), and who may submit. Without it, every borderline submission becomes an argument, and the moderator has nothing to point at when declining one.

The one run that matters

The journal guide asks you to push one article through review, because that is the question OJS has to answer. OPS has a different question, and it is answered by posting the same preprint twice.

  1. 1Submit as an authorMake yourself a second account with a different email address, and submit a made-up paper. There is no review stage to enter; it lands directly in the only stage there is.To do
  2. 2Moderate itBack as yourself: check it is in scope against what you wrote in step 5, tidy the title, authors and abstract, and post it — or decline it, which opens a discussion thread if there is anything to say.To do
  3. 3Look at what the public seesThe date stamp, the abstract, the attached file. This is the whole publication; there is no issue coming later.To do
  4. 4Post a second versionAs the author, submit a revision of the same piece. Then check the part that matters: the earlier version is still there, still reachable, and still says what it said.To do
Step 4 is the point of the exercise. Versions that stay citable are the one thing OPS does that neither the Research Hub nor a folder of PDFs can. If that behaves the way you expect, OPS works. If it does not, nothing else about it matters.

Every step sends email; watch it arrive. If none of it does, run the quick email check from the journal guide before filing anything.

Two accounts, not one

Worth its own section because it catches everybody, and because nothing on screen warns you about it.

What is separate

  • Logins. OJS and OPS keep separate lists of users. An account on one is not an account on the other, even with the same name and the same email address.
  • Passwords. Changing one does not change the other. So do resets.
  • Roles. Being a Site Administrator on the journal grants nothing here.
  • Content. Separate databases and separate files. A preprint cannot be moved into the journal by a setting; it would be submitted again.

What is shared

  • The machine, and therefore any outage. If one is down, suspect both.
  • Outgoing mail. Both send as the same address, so a message from the two looks identical in your inbox. Check which application a notification came from before acting on it — the link in the message is the reliable tell.
  • IDS, and the same ticket queue.
The practical rule: whoever administers both needs an account on each, created separately and kept in step by hand. Decide up front whether that is one person or two — it is easier to answer now than after somebody leaves.

What is OJS? What is OPS?

Open Journal Systems (OJS) is free software for running a peer-reviewed journal. Open Preprint Systems (OPS) is its sibling, built from the same code, for posting finished research straight away, without waiting for review or an issue. Both are now running on ICJIA's test server. This page says what each is, how they differ from each other and from the Research Hub, and whether OPS is worth having.

The questions this answers: what OJS is, what OPS is, how the two differ from each other and from the Research Hub, and whether OPS is worth having beside a journal. Whether either could replace the Hub is answered in the beginner's guide to the Hub; the short version is here.

A note on the word “server”. It does two different jobs on this page, and they are easy to confuse. A preprint server is a kind of publication — it is to OPS what a journal is to OJS, and it is what the role “Preprint Server Manager” refers to. A server on its own is the computer everything runs on. Nothing here asks you to think about the computer.

And a note on the names. Hub 1.0 is the Research Hub that is live now, launched in 2019, and Studio 1.0 is the tool behind it that authors use to create and publish their work. Hub 2.0 is the security update and rewrite of Hub 1.0 — the name mostly means the public front end — whose draft is finished but not yet live, pending testing and approval (its internal codename is “Copperhead”), and Studio 2.0 is the ICJIA-only publishing tool behind it — a significant upgrade over Studio 1.0. Where this page says “the Hub” without a number, it means both. If the Hub itself is unfamiliar, start with the beginner's guide.

TL;DR — the short version

Nine lines. Everything after this is the supporting detail, to be read only if you want it.

  • What OJS is. Free software for running a peer-reviewed journal, made by a non-profit and used by tens of thousands of journals. It collects submissions, sends them to reviewers, tracks the rounds and the edits, and publishes the finished articles in issues, each able to carry a permanent identifier. It is on trial here to answer whether ICJIA wants a journal of its own.
  • What OPS is. Its sibling for posting finished research straight away, with no review and no issues: an author submits, a moderator checks the piece belongs, and it is posted. Installed beside OJS on 6 September 2026 as a second, separate application — its own database, logins and web address. It took twenty minutes and hit nothing.
  • What a preprint is. A finished paper posted publicly before — or instead of — formal peer review. It is timestamped, permanently citable, and can be replaced by a newer version without losing the old one.
  • How OPS differs from OJS. No issues, no volumes, no peer-review rounds, no copyediting. An author submits, a moderator screens it for scope, and it goes up. OJS is a journal; OPS is a noticeboard with a records office attached.
  • How OPS differs from the Research Hub. Less than you would hope. Both post individual pieces continuously, as each is ready. OPS adds three things: earlier versions stay readable, each piece can carry a DOI — a permanent identifying number for a piece of research, the way an ISBN identifies a book, which keeps working even if the web address changes — and the work becomes visible to the search tools researchers actually use; it gives up the Hub's datasets, dashboards and hard-won web addresses.
  • Where Hub 2.0 fits. Not a third option: it is the security update and rewrite of Hub 1.0, with Studio 2.0 behind it, and its finished draft is not yet live, pending testing and approval. Same publishing model, content and web addresses, so it settles nothing about OJS or OPS in either direction.
  • Could OPS, or OJS, replace the Hub? No, on the evidence: two of the Hub's three kinds of content have nowhere to go, its readers do not come with it, and the in-house platform would survive anyway beside a second system. The beginner's guide to the Hub carries that argument in full, including the case for.
  • The honest verdict on adding it. OPS is the closer of the two new systems to what ICJIA already does — which is exactly why it is the harder of the two to justify. The Hub already posts research continuously and is read far more widely than a new preprint server would be.
  • Where it is worth a second look. Not as a place to publish, but as the missing front half of a journal: somewhere a working paper can get a citable identifier and a date stamp while it waits for review. That is a narrow job, and OPS does it properly.

What OJS is, and what OPS is

Two applications from one maker: the Public Knowledge Project (PKP), a non-profit that has built publishing software for universities and research bodies since 2001. Both are free, both are installed on ICJIA's test server, and they do two different jobs.

OJS — Open Journal Systems

Test install · ojs.icjia.cloud

Software for running a peer-reviewed journal. It collects submissions, sends them to reviewers, tracks the rounds and the edits, and publishes the finished articles in issues, each able to carry a permanent identifier (a DOI) and the listings that make it findable as scholarship. Tens of thousands of journals run on it.

It is a journal expressed as software: nothing publishes until there is an issue to publish it into, and everyone holds a journal role — manager, editor, reviewer, author. The proof of concept asks whether ICJIA should adopt it; the journal guide says how it is run.

OPS — Open Preprint Systems

Test install · ops.icjia.cloud

Its sibling for posting finished research straight away, without waiting for peer review or an issue. An author submits, a moderator checks that the piece belongs, and it is posted with a date stamp, a DOI if the server registers them, and every later version kept beside the first.

The same code underneath, one stage instead of four, and it labels everything it holds a preprint. It was installed beside OJS on 6 September 2026 as a second, separate application; the preprint server guide says how it is run. The rest of this page is mostly about whether it is worth having.

OJS and OPS: the same machine, geared differently

These two are not competitors and not really alternatives. They are the same software underneath — the Public Knowledge Project builds both from one shared foundation — set up for two different jobs. Seeing the workflows next to each other makes the difference obvious in a way a feature list does not.

OJS — a journal

Four stages. The finish line is an issue.

  • SubmissionAuthor submits; an editor decides whether it goes forward at all.
  • ReviewReviewers are invited, rounds are run, deadlines chased, names hidden if the journal wants them hidden.
  • CopyeditingLanguage, references and house style, with the author signing off.
  • ProductionFinal files made, then scheduled into an issue and published with the rest of it.

Nothing publishes until there is an issue to publish it into. That is not a limitation to be worked around — it is the definition of a periodical, and it is what OJS is for.

OPS — a preprint server

One stage. The finish line is the post.

  • Submission → ProductionA submission lands directly in the only stage there is. There is no review stage to enter, because there is no review.
  • ModerationA moderator checks that it is in scope and appropriate, tidies up the title, authors and summary, and posts it — or declines it, with a discussion thread if there is anything to say.
  • PostedPublic immediately, with a date stamp, and a new version whenever there is a revision to post.

PKP is careful about the vocabulary here, and so is this document: OPS posts, it does not publish. The distinction is the entire point. A moderator is not an editor, and screening is not review. The description OPS attaches to everything it posts says so: every item is labeled a preprint, permanently and by design.

The practical consequence: a preprint server cannot be quietly used as a journal, and a journal cannot be quietly used as a preprint server. The software will let you try; the description of the work that reaches Crossref (the non-profit that registers those permanent numbers), Google Scholar and the library catalogs will say which it is either way.

What a preprint actually is

The word does a lot of work, and it is worth getting right before comparing anything. A preprint is a complete piece of research, written up and made public by its authors, before it has been through peer review — or in place of it. It is not a draft, not a summary, and not a work in progress. It is the finished thing, published early.

Three properties do the work

  • A date stamp that holds up. Posting establishes publicly, and permanently, that this work existed on this date. In fields where two teams can be working on the same question, that matters.
  • A permanent identifier. A preprint can carry a DOI of its own, so it can be cited in other people's work — and cited correctly — long before any journal accepts it.
  • Versions, kept. A revision goes up as a new version. The old one does not disappear; anyone who cited it still finds what they cited. When a journal eventually publishes the work, the preprint points at that final version.

Take those three away and a preprint is just a document on a website. They are the whole reason preprint software exists rather than a folder of PDFs.

Three names. Three jobs.

Now the Research Hub, because the question about OPS is really a question about it. The three names below are easy to mix up, and one of them is not a new system at all. This is the whole distinction, and the rest of the page is only evidence for it.

Hub 1.0 and Hub 2.0Hub 1.0 is live now; Hub 2.0 is the rewrite, draft finished, not yet live

Getting research read.

Where ICJIA research reaches people, and by a wide margin: 60% of everyone who visits the agency's website. Articles, datasets and dashboards, each published as it is finished by authors working in the Studio behind it.

Hub 2.0 is this same thing, rebuilt — not a third system. A major upgrade on a supported platform, with Studio 2.0 behind it; what it deliberately does not change is the publishing model — same content, same continuous posting, same name, same web addresses. So it belongs in this row.

OPSOpen Preprint Systems

Getting research cited.

The same continuous publishing as the Hub, plus the things that make work count as scholarship: a permanent identifier, versions that stay citable, and visibility to Google Scholar.

But it starts with nothing the Hub has. No datasets or dashboards as publications, no readers, no ranked web addresses — and it labels everything it holds a preprint, permanently.

OJSOpen Journal Systems

Getting research reviewed.

The only one of the three that reviews anything. Outside reviewers, rounds and deadlines, then issues published on a schedule, each article carrying a DOI.

It is a journal, not a website. Nothing publishes until there is an issue to publish it into, which is the definition of a periodical rather than a limitation.

If you read nothing else on this page: Hub 2.0 is the Hub you already have, rebuilt and much improved — which is why it settles nothing about the other two, in either direction. OPS and OJS are additions to whichever Hub is running. And only OJS does something no version of the Hub can be adjusted into doing.

Where OJS, OPS and the Hub overlap

This is the section that decides the matter, so here it is in one sentence: three of the four do the same job, and only OJS does a different one. Hub 1.0, Hub 2.0 and OPS are all continuous publishers — work goes public when it is finished, approved by one person. OJS is the odd one out: it gathers reviewed work into issues, on a schedule.

First: Hub 2.0 is not a separate option

A major upgrade to the Hub — but not a different way of publishing

This is the point most easily misread, so it is worth being blunt about. Hub 2.0 does not sit in a third place on this page. It publishes the same three kinds of content, continuously, with the same single approval, under the same name, at the same web addresses. For readers it works exactly as it does now, in a modern interface: it was built to fix security, not to add or remove features. On the questions this page asks, most of its column reads “no change”.

On the question this page is about — how ICJIA publishes — Hub 1.0 and Hub 2.0 fall in the same place. Hub 2.0 is a better, safer, more capable version of the same publishing model, which is exactly what it was meant to be. And that is why it settles nothing about OJS or OPS in either direction: whichever Hub is running, the two questions in the next box are answered the same way.

What the three continuous publishers already share

  • All three post individual pieces continuously, each when it is finished, with nothing waiting on anything else.
  • All three put a draft in front of one person who approves it before it goes public — a manager in the Studio, a moderator in OPS.
  • All three keep authors, abstracts, topics and attached files as structured information rather than as prose on a page.
  • All three produce a public, searchable library of ICJIA research that stays put and can be cited.

Four out of four. This is the uncomfortable part of the OPS question: OPS is not a strange new category — it is very close to what ICJIA already does, and being close to the incumbent is a far harder position to argue from than being different from it.

Two questions separate all four

Everything else is detail. If you remember nothing else from this page, remember these two.

  • 1. Is the work formally reviewed by outside experts?
    Only OJS. Not Hub 1.0, not Hub 2.0, not OPS. A manager's approval, a moderator's screening and a comment on a passage are all useful, and none of them is peer review. This is the single thing no version of the Hub can be adjusted into providing without becoming a journal.
  • 2. Is it registered as scholarship? — a permanent identifier, versions that stay citable, and visibility to Google Scholar and citation counts.
    OJS and OPS, yes. Neither version of the Hub. Hub articles are read by tens of thousands of people and are close to invisible to the scholarly record. That is a real ceiling on work the agency has already paid to produce, and Hub 2.0 does not set out to lift it.

There is a third thing, and it runs the other way: only the Hub has readers. 60% of the agency's visitors, 84% of its search traffic, fifteen minutes a visit, and roughly 540 web addresses ranked by Google over seven years. Neither OJS nor OPS starts with any of that, which is where the next section begins.

So where does each fall? The Hub, in both versions, is where ICJIA's research reaches people — and Hub 2.0 is that same job done on a current, secure, more capable platform. OPS is the Hub's shape plus a scholarly record: versions, DOIs, indexing, minus the datasets, dashboards and readers. OJS is the only one that reviews anything, and the only one that produces a periodical.

Could OPS, or OJS, replace the Hub?

This is the question most often asked about the preprint server, and it is answered in full in What is the Research Hub?, where it belongs: the case for retiring an in-house platform put at full strength, what removal would actually mean, and a costed look at extending the Studio instead. The short version, so that this page stands on its own:

No, on the evidence. Neither system can hold a dataset or a dashboard as a publication, so two of the Hub's three kinds of content have nowhere to go. Into OPS every article becomes a preprint, permanently and in a form machines read — a false statement about finished work. Moving roughly 540 addresses puts 84% of the agency's search traffic at risk. And off-the-shelf software is still a server to run, so the in-house platform would survive for what could not move, next to a second system. The worry behind the question — software only ICJIA maintains, unpatched since 2022 — is real, and Hub 2.0 answers the second half of it.

If not instead of the Hub, is OPS worth having alongside it?

On its own, and as the front half of a journal

Hard to justify on its own

This is the honest answer, and it is not the flattering one. The Hub already does continuous posting, and does it for an audience OPS would have to build from nothing. Running a second continuous-posting system means a second thing to keep patched, backed up, and staffed — in exchange for versioning and indexing that a smaller change to the Studio might also deliver.

Where the argument gets interesting is not publication at all — it is the front half of a journal. If ICJIA ever runs the quarterly the proof of concept describes, there is a real gap between “a working paper exists” and “an issue comes out six months later.” OPS fills exactly that gap: the paper gets posted, dated, versioned and citable straight away, and when the reviewed version appears in the journal, the preprint points at it. That is the job OPS was built for, and the one place it is clearly the right tool rather than a near-duplicate of something ICJIA already owns.

Which makes OPS contingent on the journal, not independent of it. If the quarterly does not happen, the case for OPS largely goes with it.

The recommendation: keep OPS installed for the length of the proof of concept — it costs nothing extra to run and it is the cheapest way to see the workflow honestly. Do not treat it as a separate decision. Decide the journal first; OPS is a sensible companion to a quarterly and hard to defend without one.

What is actually running

Installed 6 September 2026 on the same server as the OJS test, sharing its database system, the software that serves web pages, and its outgoing mail. What is not shared is deliberate: the two applications cannot share one database, so each has its own, with its own folder of uploaded files and its own user accounts.

One consequence worth knowing before anyone is given a login: accounts do not carry across. OJS and OPS keep separate user tables, so whoever administers both needs an account on each, created separately and kept in step by hand. Both send as the same shared administrative address, so mail from the two looks identical to a recipient — which is convenient today and worth revisiting if the two ever need to be managed by different people.

Nothing here is permanent

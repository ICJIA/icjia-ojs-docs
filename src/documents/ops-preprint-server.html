A preprint server, next to the journal

Open Preprint Systems (OPS) is now running alongside OJS on the same test server. It is built by the same people, from the same code, and it does a deliberately smaller job: it posts finished research publicly, straight away, without waiting for peer review or an issue.

TL;DR — the short version

Seven lines. Everything after this is the supporting detail, to be read only if you want it.

  • What was installed. Open Preprint Systems 3.5.0-5, the preprint sibling of OJS, on the same droplet and the same MySQL server. Separate database, separate logins, its own address. It took twenty minutes and hit nothing.
  • What a preprint is. A finished paper posted publicly before — or instead of — formal peer review. It is timestamped, permanently citable, and can be replaced by a newer version without losing the old one.
  • How OPS differs from OJS. No issues, no volumes, no peer-review rounds, no copyediting. An author submits, a moderator screens it for scope, and it goes up. OJS is a journal; OPS is a noticeboard with a records office attached.
  • How OPS differs from the Research Hub. Less than you would hope. Both post individual pieces continuously, as each is ready. OPS adds versioning, DOIs and scholarly indexing; it gives up the Hub's datasets, dashboards and hard-won web addresses.
  • Where Hub 2.0 “Copperhead” fits. It is not a third option. It is the Hub you already have, rebuilt on software that still receives security updates — same content, same continuous publishing, same web addresses. It settles nothing about OJS or OPS, in either direction.
  • The honest verdict. OPS is the closest of the three to what ICJIA already does — which is exactly why it is the hardest of the three to justify. The Hub already posts research continuously and is read far more widely than a new preprint server would be.
  • Where it is worth a second look. Not as a place to publish, but as the missing front half of a journal: somewhere a working paper can get a citable identifier and a date stamp while it waits for review. That is a narrow job, and OPS does it properly.

Read on for the side-by-side comparison, the overlap with the Hub stated plainly, and what is actually running today.

Three names. Two jobs.

Before any detail: the three names in this document are easy to mix up, and one of them is not a new system at all. This is the whole distinction, and the rest of the page is only evidence for it.

The Research HubIncluding Hub 2.0, codenamed Copperhead

Getting research read.

Where ICJIA research reaches people, and by a wide margin: 60% of everyone who visits the agency's website. Articles, datasets and dashboards, each published as it is finished.

Copperhead is this, rebuilt — not a third system. Same content, same continuous publishing, same name and web addresses. What changes is the software underneath, which stopped getting security updates in 2022. It changes the plumbing, not the publishing.

OPSOpen Preprint Systems

Getting research cited.

The same continuous publishing as the Hub, plus the things that make work count as scholarship: a permanent identifier, versions that stay citable, and visibility to Google Scholar.

But it starts with nothing the Hub has. No datasets or dashboards as publications, no readers, no ranked web addresses — and it labels everything it holds a preprint, permanently.

OJSOpen Journal Systems

Getting research reviewed.

The only one of the three that reviews anything. Outside reviewers, rounds and deadlines, then issues published on a schedule, each article carrying a DOI.

It is a journal, not a website. Nothing publishes until there is an issue to publish it into, which is the definition of a periodical rather than a limitation.

If you read nothing else on this page: Copperhead is the Hub you already have, rebuilt — it settles nothing about the other two, in either direction. OPS and OJS are additions. And only OJS does something no version of the Hub can be adjusted into doing.

What a preprint actually is

The word does a lot of work, and it is worth getting right before comparing anything. A preprint is a complete piece of research, written up and made public by its authors, before it has been through peer review — or in place of it. It is not a draft, not a summary, and not a work in progress. It is the finished thing, published early.

Three properties do the work

  • A date stamp that holds up. Posting establishes publicly, and permanently, that this work existed on this date. In fields where two teams can be working the same question, that matters.
  • A permanent identifier. A preprint can carry a DOI of its own, so it can be cited in other people's work — and cited correctly — long before any journal accepts it.
  • Versions, kept. Authors post a revision as a new version. The old one does not disappear; anyone who cited it still finds what they cited. When a journal eventually publishes the work, the preprint points at that final version.

Take those three away and a preprint is just a document on a website. They are the whole reason preprint software exists rather than a folder of PDFs.

OJS and OPS: the same machine, geared differently

These two are not competitors and not really alternatives. They are the same software underneath — the Public Knowledge Project builds both from one shared codebase — configured for two different jobs. Seeing the workflows next to each other makes the difference obvious in a way a feature list does not.

OJS — a journal

Four stages. The finish line is an issue.

  • SubmissionAuthor submits; an editor decides whether it goes forward at all.
  • ReviewReviewers are invited, rounds are run, deadlines chased, names hidden if the journal wants them hidden.
  • CopyeditingLanguage, references and house style, with the author signing off.
  • ProductionFinal files made, then scheduled into an issue and published with the rest of it.

Nothing publishes until there is an issue to publish it into. That is not a limitation to be worked around — it is the definition of a periodical, and it is what OJS is for.

OPS — a preprint server

One stage. The finish line is the post.

  • Submission → ProductionA submission lands directly in the only stage there is. There is no review stage to enter, because there is no review.
  • ModerationA moderator checks that it is in scope and appropriate, tidies the metadata, and posts it — or declines it, with a discussion thread if there is anything to say.
  • PostedPublic immediately, with a date stamp, and a new version whenever the authors have one.

PKP is careful about the vocabulary here, and so is this document: OPS posts, it does not publish. The distinction is the entire point. A moderator is not an editor and screening is not review, and the metadata OPS produces says so — every item it emits is labelled a preprint, permanently and by design.

The practical consequence: a preprint server cannot be quietly used as a journal, and a journal cannot be quietly used as a preprint server. The software will let you try; the metadata that reaches Crossref, Google Scholar and the library catalogues will tell the truth either way.

Where OJS, OPS and the Hub overlap

This is the section that decides the matter, so here it is in one sentence: three of the four do the same job, and only OJS does a different one. The Hub as it runs today, the Hub 2.0 rebuild and OPS are all continuous publishers — work goes public when it is finished, approved by one person. OJS is the odd one out: it gathers reviewed work into issues, on a schedule.

First: Copperhead is not a separate option

It is the Hub, rebuilt — not a different way of publishing

This is the point most easily misread, so it is worth being blunt about. Research Hub 2.0 does not sit in a third place on this page. It publishes the same three kinds of content, continuously, with the same single approval, under the same name, at the same web addresses. Every row of the table above says “same” except two.

What it does change is the foundation, and that change is the whole reason it exists: current, supported software in place of a system that stopped receiving security updates in 2022. The new Studio also lets an editor highlight a passage and leave a comment on it, which is a real improvement on a single yes-or-no approval — but it is a better conversation, not peer review.

So for the purposes of this comparison, Hub 1.0 and Hub 2.0 fall in the same place. That is not a criticism of Copperhead. Fixing the foundation was the job, and it is the most urgent of anything described on this page. It simply means choosing it is not choosing a different publishing model — which is exactly why it settles nothing about OJS or OPS, in either direction.

What the three continuous publishers already share

  • All three post individual pieces continuously, each when it is finished, with nothing waiting on anything else.
  • All three put a draft in front of one person who approves it before it goes public — a manager in the Studio, a moderator in OPS.
  • All three keep authors, abstracts, topics and attached files as structured information rather than as prose on a page.
  • All three produce a public, searchable library of ICJIA research that stays put and can be cited.

Four out of four. This is the uncomfortable part and the reason this document exists: OPS is not a strange new category — it is very close to what ICJIA already does, and being close to the incumbent is a far harder position to argue from than being different to it.

Two questions separate all four

Everything else is detail. If you remember nothing else from this page, remember these two.

  • 1. Is the work formally reviewed by outside experts?
    Only OJS. Not the Hub today, not Hub 2.0, not OPS. A manager's approval, a moderator's screening and a comment on a passage are all useful, and none of them is peer review. This is the single thing no version of the Hub can be adjusted into providing without becoming a journal.
  • 2. Is it registered as scholarship? — a permanent identifier, versions that stay citable, and visibility to Google Scholar and citation counts.
    OJS and OPS, yes. Neither version of the Hub. Hub articles are read by tens of thousands of people and are close to invisible to the scholarly record. That is a real ceiling on work the agency has already paid to produce, and Copperhead does not lift it.

There is a third thing, and it runs the other way: only the Hub has readers. 60% of the agency's visitors, 84% of its search traffic, fifteen minutes a visit, and roughly 540 web addresses ranked by Google over six years. Neither OJS nor OPS starts with any of that, and neither can hold a dataset or a dashboard as a publication in its own right. Two of the Hub's three kinds of content do not survive a move to either one.

So where does each fall? The Hub, in both versions, is where ICJIA's research reaches people — and Copperhead is that same job on software that is still supported. OPS is the Hub's shape plus a scholarly record: versions, DOIs, indexing, minus the datasets, dashboards and readers. OJS is the only one that reviews anything, and the only one that produces a periodical.

So does ICJIA need it?

As a replacement for the Research Hub?

No — for the same reasons as OJS, and one more

Everything the proof of concept says about OJS replacing the Hub applies here: the datasets and dashboards have nowhere to go, and every one of roughly 540 web addresses would move, at a cost the traffic figures make plain.

OPS adds a reason of its own. Its metadata declares every item a preprint — that is not a setting, it is what the software is. Publishing ICJIA's finished, authoritative research as preprints would describe the agency's work as provisional when it is not. That is worse than a technical mismatch; it is a factually wrong claim about the work, made permanently and machine-readably.

As a place to post research alongside the Hub?

Hard to justify on its own

This is the honest answer, and it is not the flattering one. The Hub already does continuous posting, and does it for an audience OPS would have to build from nothing. Running a second continuous-posting system means a second thing to keep patched, backed up, and staffed — in exchange for versioning and indexing that a smaller change to the Studio might also deliver.

Where the argument gets interesting is not publication at all — it is the front half of a journal. If ICJIA ever runs the quarterly the proof of concept describes, there is a real gap between “a working paper exists” and “an issue comes out six months later.” OPS fills exactly that gap: the paper gets posted, dated, versioned and citable straight away, and when the reviewed version appears in the journal, the preprint points at it. That is the job OPS was built for, and the one place it is clearly the right tool rather than a near-duplicate of something ICJIA already owns.

Which makes OPS contingent on the journal, not independent of it. If the quarterly does not happen, the case for OPS largely goes with it.

The recommendation: keep OPS installed for the length of the proof of concept — it costs nothing extra to run and it is the cheapest way to see the workflow honestly. Do not treat it as a separate decision. Decide the journal first; OPS is a sensible companion to a quarterly and hard to defend without one.

What is actually running

Installed 6 September 2026 on the same droplet as the OJS test, sharing its MySQL server, its PHP, its web server and its outgoing mail. What is not shared is deliberate: the two applications use overlapping table names, so each has its own database, its own files directory, and its own user accounts.

One consequence worth knowing before anyone is given a login: accounts do not carry across. OJS and OPS keep separate user tables, so whoever administers both needs an account on each, created separately and kept in step by hand. Both send as the same shared administrative address, so mail from the two looks identical to a recipient — which is convenient today and worth revisiting if the two ever need to be managed by different people.

How it was installed

The droplet runbook carries the full second-site procedure — DNS, the web-server configuration copied from OJS, the separate database, the tarball, and the memory tuning both applications needed once they shared one small server.

How the workflow is run

The administrator guide covers OJS. Most of it transfers: the two share their settings screens, their user management and their email templates. What does not transfer is everything about review and issues — in OPS, none of it is there.

Nothing here is permanent

OPS is on the same temporary server as the journal test, at a temporary address, and it goes when that goes.

Nothing posted to it is a record of anything. No DOIs are registered, no preprint is public, and no ICJIA research has been put on it.

Do not post anything to it you would mind losing.
